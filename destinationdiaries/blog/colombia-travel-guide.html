Colombia Off the Beaten Path: Beyond Cartagena & Medellín

Priya NairOctober 13, 2026 · 12 min read

Cartagena and Medellín are wonderful. They're also now firmly on the global tourist circuit, with prices and crowds to match. Colombia's real character — the warmth, the wildness, the extraordinary natural diversity — lives somewhere else. Here's where to find it.

The Coffee Region (Eje Cafetero)

The coffee triangle of Caldas, Quindío, and Risaralda produces some of the world's finest coffee in a landscape of steep green hills and colonial haciendas. Salento is the most accessible entry point — a beautiful small town with a famous multi-colored main square and trails into the Valle de Cocora, where Colombia's national tree (the wax palm) grows to 60 meters against misty cloud forest.

Spend three days in the coffee region. Visit one of the traditional fincas (estates) that offers tours — not the commercial ones near Salento, but smaller family operations in the hills around Manizales or Armenia where you see the full process and stay for lunch. The Colombian coffee you drink in Bogotá is dramatically better than what gets exported.

Ciudad Perdida (The Lost City)

The Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta is one of the world's highest coastal mountain ranges, and hidden within it at 1,300 meters altitude is Ciudad Perdida — a pre-Columbian city of terraces and stairways built 650 years before Machu Picchu. The only way to reach it is a 4-day jungle trek with a licensed indigenous guide.

The trek is genuinely challenging — river crossings, steep jungle paths in oppressive heat and humidity. The reward is arriving at the stone terraces that most people in the world don't know exist, with views down into the jungle canopy. The indigenous Kogi community who guide the trail regard it as sacred. They are right to.

Nuquí and the Pacific Coast

The Colombian Pacific coast is one of South America's last genuine frontiers for travelers. Nuquí is accessible only by light aircraft from Medellín (30 minutes), which keeps visitor numbers low. What you find: humpback whale migration from July to November, empty Pacific beaches, one of the world's highest biodiversity zones, and Afro-Colombian communities with food and music traditions unlike anything in the Caribbean coast cities.

The Tatacoa Desert

Two hours south of Bogotá by road, Colombia has a semi-arid desert — the Desierto de la Tatacoa — that looks like it belongs in Mars more than the tropics. Deep red and grey eroded rock formations, the silence of the desert, and some of the best stargazing in South America. Almost no tourists.

  • Most AdventurousCiudad Perdida trek (4 days)
  • Best WildlifeNuquí (humpbacks July–November)
  • CurrencyColombian Peso (COP) — ~4,000 COP = $1
  • Average Daily Budget$35–60 (mid-range)
  • Best Month to VisitDecember–March (dry season)
