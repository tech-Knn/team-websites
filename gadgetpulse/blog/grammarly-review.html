Grammarly Review 2026: Is It Still Worth the Price After 6 Months of Real Use?

By Priya Nair October 30, 2026 12 min read 41 Comments

We subscribed to Grammarly Premium for six months, used it across email, long-form writing, coding documentation, and social content — and compared it against free alternatives including LanguageTool and ChatGPT. Here's the honest truth about what it does well, where it falls short, and whether the price tag is justified.

Grammarly launched in 2009 as a simple spelling and grammar checker. In 2026, it has evolved into a full AI writing assistant — complete with tone detection, clarity rewrites, a built-in plagiarism checker, and a generative AI feature called "Grammarly GO" that can draft entire emails or paragraphs on demand. The free plan still exists and is genuinely useful. But the Premium plan at $30/month (or $144/year on annual billing) is where Grammarly either justifies itself or doesn't.

What Grammarly Premium Actually Does

The free Grammarly catches basic spelling errors and obvious grammar mistakes — the kind of stuff your phone keyboard already catches. Premium unlocks the features that make the tool genuinely powerful:

  • Advanced grammar and style suggestions — catches passive voice overuse, unclear pronoun references, wordiness, and sentences that are technically correct but confusing
  • Tone detector — analyzes whether your writing sounds confident, formal, friendly, or aggressive, and flags mismatches for the context
  • Clarity rewrites — offers full-sentence rewrites when a sentence is grammatically correct but hard to read
  • Plagiarism checker — scans against 16 billion web pages; essential for students, journalists, and content marketers
  • Full-document consistency checks — catches inconsistent hyphenation, formatting, and terminology across long documents
  • Grammarly GO — the generative AI layer that can write, rephrase, or expand content on command

Performance: What Actually Impressed Us

01

Real-Time Browser Integration Is Seamless

The Chrome extension works in virtually every text box on the web — Gmail, LinkedIn, Notion, Google Docs, HubSpot, WordPress, Slack. Suggestions appear inline without interrupting your flow. After six months, it genuinely felt like a natural part of typing rather than an external tool. No other writing assistant we tested came close to this level of integration depth.

02

Tone Detection Is Surprisingly Accurate

We tested tone detection by deliberately writing emails in ambiguous registers — a rejection email phrased too bluntly, a follow-up that read as passive-aggressive, a professional message that came across as condescending. Grammarly flagged all three correctly and offered rewrites that genuinely improved the tone without losing the core message. This feature alone saves multiple re-reads on important emails.

03

Clarity Rewrites Are the Best Feature

When you write a sentence that is technically correct but confusing — complex nested clauses, double negatives, buried verbs — Grammarly's clarity suggestions offer a rewrite at the click of a button. We accepted approximately 60% of these suggestions without modification. The remaining 40% were wrong for our context, but the starting point was always useful. This is where Grammarly clearly separates itself from LanguageTool and basic spell-checkers.

Grammarly's real-time suggestions integrate across browsers, desktop apps, and Google Docs.

Where Grammarly Falls Short

Grammarly GO Is Not a ChatGPT Replacement

Grammarly's generative AI feature — GO — can draft emails, rephrase paragraphs, and create outlines. But compared to Claude or ChatGPT, the output quality is noticeably weaker. The drafts are safe and grammatically clean but rarely interesting. For high-stakes creative writing or detailed content generation, you will still need a dedicated AI tool. Grammarly GO is better understood as a quick-draft shortcut for routine communications, not a full writing AI.

It Misses Industry-Specific Terminology

Grammarly's general-purpose language model occasionally flags correct technical terminology as errors — medical, legal, software, and marketing jargon regularly trigger false suggestions. You can add words to your personal dictionary, but this is a friction point for professionals in specialized fields. We added 47 custom words across six months of use — a minor but real annoyance.

The Plagiarism Checker Has Coverage Gaps

The plagiarism checker's 16 billion page index sounds impressive. In practice, we found it missed paraphrased content effectively and sometimes flagged common phrases as suspicious. For academic integrity checks, dedicated tools like Turnitin remain more thorough. For basic content marketing use cases, Grammarly's checker is adequate.

Grammarly Free vs Premium: The Honest Comparison

The free plan covers spelling, basic grammar, and punctuation — genuinely useful for anyone. If you write fewer than 2,000 words per week for professional purposes, the free plan is likely sufficient. Premium starts justifying its cost when you write regularly for work: email-heavy roles, content creation, client-facing communications, or any job where writing quality directly affects your credibility.

At $30/month on monthly billing, Premium is hard to justify for casual use. At $12/month on annual billing, it becomes much more defensible. The Business plan ($15/person/month on annual) adds a team style guide and centralised billing, which is worth it for content or marketing teams of five or more.

Pros
  • Best browser integration of any writing tool
  • Accurate tone detection and clarity rewrites
  • Works everywhere — email, Google Docs, Notion, Slack
  • Consistency checks across long documents
  • Plagiarism checker included in Premium
Cons
  • Monthly plan is expensive at $30/month
  • Grammarly GO is weaker than dedicated AI tools
  • False flags on technical/industry jargon
  • Plagiarism checker misses paraphrasing
  • Free plan feels deliberately limited to push upgrades

The Bottom Line

Grammarly Premium is a genuinely excellent writing assistant for professionals who communicate in writing regularly. The browser integration is best-in-class, the clarity suggestions are worth the price alone, and the tone detection prevents embarrassing miscommunications before they happen. It is not a replacement for a dedicated AI writing tool, and it is overpriced on monthly billing. Buy the annual plan or the Business tier, and you'll get real value. Casual writers and students can likely get by with the free plan or LanguageTool's free tier.

"After six months with Grammarly Premium, my editing time on professional emails dropped by roughly 40%. That's not a marketing number — that's what we tracked. The annual plan at $144 pays for itself in recovered time within the first two months for a heavy business writer."

Our recommendation: try the free plan for two weeks and count how many times you wish a suggestion had gone deeper. If you hit that wall regularly, the annual Premium plan is worth it. If the free plan covers your needs, don't upgrade.
